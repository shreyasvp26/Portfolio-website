## The problem

Every project above this one is mine. This one isn't, and that's why it's here.

Shreepad Seva Mandal is a nonprofit whose app is used by an actual community — people who are not engineers, don't read changelogs, and can't be asked to reinstall a build to work around your bug. Three developers maintain it across iOS and Android; I lead the iOS side and contribute to Android where parity demands it. There is no QA team, no release manager, and no beta population large enough to catch everything, so the failure modes are the ones you only meet in production, on devices you don't own.

Student projects rarely teach this. A course project is finished when it works on your laptop. An app in the store is never finished, and the skill it demands isn't writing features — it's not breaking things for people who trust you.

## Architecture: one web app, two native shells

The app is a Next.js application running inside a Capacitor native shell, shipped as a real binary to both stores. For a three-person team maintaining two platforms, that's the decision that makes the whole thing tractable — but it relocates the difficulty rather than removing it.

<Decision
  choice="Ship a Next.js app inside a Capacitor shell rather than writing native iOS and Android clients."
  because="Three engineers cannot maintain feature parity across two native codebases. One web codebase means a bug fixed is a bug fixed everywhere, and shipping a UI change doesn't require two implementations by two people who then have to agree."
  cost="You inherit two native build systems anyway, and every capability that touches the device — notifications, authentication redirects, storage, permissions — runs through a plugin whose behaviour genuinely differs per platform. The shared layer is the easy 80%; the remaining 20% is where all the bugs live, and it's the part you can't write once."
/>

What surprised me is where the boundary actually falls. Product logic and UI became almost entirely platform-agnostic, which is the promised benefit and it's real. But the *native bridge* is not one API with two implementations — it's two platform behaviours behind a shared function signature, and the signature hides differences that matter.

## The release pipeline

I own the path from a merged change to a user's phone. On iOS that path has a gatekeeper you cannot argue with, and that changes how you work.

<Diagram
  name="ssm-release"
  caption="The iOS release path. App Store review is a hard gate with human latency — a mistake caught after submission costs days, not minutes — so verification has to happen before the archive is uploaded, not after."
/>

The asymmetry shapes everything. On the web, a bad deploy is a rollback and you're done in minutes. On iOS, a bad release is a new build, a new submission, a new review queue, and a rollout you have to nurse. That single fact pushes cost backwards through the entire process: the cheapest place to catch a bug is before upload, so TestFlight has to carry the weight that a rollback button carries elsewhere.

<Decision
  choice="Treat TestFlight external testing as a mandatory gate, not an optional courtesy."
  because="Review latency makes post-submission fixes expensive in wall-clock days. Real devices in testers' hands surface the OS-version and device-specific problems a simulator won't, and in a Capacitor app that includes WebView differences across iOS versions."
  cost="Every release carries a built-in delay before it can even be submitted, so shipping a small fix takes days rather than hours. Genuinely frustrating, and still cheaper than an expedited-review request against a broken build."
/>

## Implementation and trade-offs

**Authentication is the worst class of production bug.** A user who can't log in has no path to tell you about it — they stop opening the app, and you never hear from them. The failure is silent by construction, so you can't rely on user reports and have to reason about the state machine directly: what happens to a session that expires while the app is backgrounded, what happens when a token was invalidated server-side, what happens when the same person signs in on a second device.

Capacitor adds a wrinkle that pure-web auth doesn't have. An auth flow that leaves the WebView and comes back — the usual shape of a redirect-based login — depends on the native shell handing control back correctly, and "correctly" is a per-platform detail. A flow that works perfectly in a desktop browser can strand a user on a blank screen inside the app.

**Push notifications are two systems pretending to be one.** This was the most instructive part. A single product feature — "notify users" — is implemented by two entirely different platform mechanisms with different permission models, payload shapes, delivery guarantees, and silent-failure modes. iOS requires explicit permission that a user can revoke in Settings without the app being told in any obvious way, and a device token that rotates.

Notifications are also the easiest thing in mobile to *believe* you've fixed, because **a successful send is not a successful delivery**. Nothing errors when a message quietly goes nowhere. That means the only trustworthy verification is a real device actually receiving it, which is slow and unglamorous and the only thing that works.

**Parity is an engineering constraint, not a QA checkbox.** I lead iOS, but I also work in the Android side, and I'd argue that's necessary rather than generous. Parity bugs are almost never "Android is broken" — they're "the two platforms disagree", and you can't diagnose a disagreement from one side of it. Patching the symptom only on iOS produces two shells that drift, and drift is how one small inconsistency becomes two divergent products maintained by three people.

<Callout tone="insight" title="What this project taught me that the others didn't">
My solo projects taught me architecture and modelling. This one taught me the cost of being wrong in front of people who never consented to being test subjects — and that a large share of production engineering is arranging things so the expensive mistakes are the hard ones to make.
</Callout>

## Outcomes

The app is [live on the App Store](https://apps.apple.com/in/app/shreepad-seva-mandal/id6788840546) and actively maintained. Releases go out through a pipeline I own, and production issues in authentication, notifications, and cross-platform behaviour have been diagnosed and fixed rather than worked around. The organisation gets a working app; I got the experience of shipping software where the users are strangers and the review gate has no undo.
